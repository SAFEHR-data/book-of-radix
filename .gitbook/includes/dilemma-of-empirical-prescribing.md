---
title: Dilemma of Empirical prescribing
---

Optimal antibiotic prescribing requires matching the antibiotic to the pathogen: too narrow risks treatment failure; too broad drives resistance, causing harm to both the individual patient and to society. Ideally, prescribing decisions would be guided by confirmed microbiological diagnosis and susceptibility testing. In practice, several factors make this difficult to achieve.

Microbiology results take days to return, so initial treatment decisions are necessarily empirical. A patient's prior antibiotic exposure and resistance history are therefore critical to guide therapy, but this information is frequently fragmented across multiple providers on non-interoperable systems and is not readily accessible at the point of prescribing. Even where data are available, their volume and complexity can impede timely decision-making, particularly in emergency settings. Most prescribing decisions are made by generalists rather than infection specialists, and the nuanced judgement required to balance immediate clinical need against the longer-term risk of resistance selection is not always available at the bedside.

As a result, prescribers rely on heuristics and local guidelines rather than patient- specific data. Although infection specialist input can be sought, the volume of cases makes individual review impractical at scale. Empirical treatment is therefore frequently initiated without full consideration of patient-specific risk factors.
