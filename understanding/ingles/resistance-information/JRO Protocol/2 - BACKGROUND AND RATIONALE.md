# 2. BACKGROUND AND RATIONALE

## 2.1 The global and national burden of antimicrobial resistance

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the most serious threats to global health. In 2019, bacterial AMR was directly attributable to an estimated 1.27 million deaths worldwide and associated with a further 4.95 million deaths; AMR mortality exceeded that of HIV/AIDS or malaria in the same period (Murray et al. 2022). Updated estimates for 2021 indicate 1.14 million deaths directly attributable to AMR and 4.71 million associated deaths, with projections suggesting these figures will rise to 1.91 million and 8.22 million respectively by 2050 without further intervention (Naghavi et al. 2024). The United Kingdom has responded with successive national action plans (HM Government et al., n.d.; Holmes et al. 2016). In England, antibiotic-resistant bacteraemia episodes totalled 20,484 in 2024, a 13.1% increase since 2019, with London carrying the highest regional burden (UKHSA 2024).

## 2.2 The burden of urinary tract infection in the NHS

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most common indications for antibiotic prescribing in both primary and secondary care. They account for 1–3% of all general practice consultations in the UK and are the second most common reason for antibiotic prescribing in English primary care, accounting for approximately one fifth of antibiotic prescriptions between 2013 and 2015 (Dolk et al. 2018). In primary care, the majority of patients with a UTI consultation receive antibiotics on the same day (UKHSA 2023, n.d.).

In secondary care, UTIs span a spectrum from uncomplicated lower urinary tract symptoms to pyelonephritis and urosepsis. In elderly patients, deferred or absent antibiotic treatment for UTI is associated with a significantly increased risk of bloodstream infection and all-cause mortality (Gharbi et al. 2019).

The microbiological importance of UTIs extends well beyond the urinary tract. *Enterobacterales* accounted for 80.6% of urinary isolates in England in 2024, with *Escherichia coli* comprising approximately 70% of urinary *Enterobacterales* episodes and *Klebsiella pneumoniae* a further 7.4% (UKHSA 2024). At the systemic level, *Enterobacterales* (principally *E. coli*, *K. pneumoniae*, and *K. oxytoca*) constituted 85.1% of all antibiotic-resistant bacteraemia cases in England in 2024 (UKHSA 2024). *E. coli* bacteraemia carries a 30-day case fatality rate of 15.9% and a mortality rate of 10.9 deaths per 100,000 population, with a urinary source in 45.0% of cases (UKHSA 2022–23; UKHSA 2023–24). The clinical and economic burden of UTI in the NHS is therefore substantial, and the consequences of inadequate treatment extend well beyond the urinary tract.

## 2.3 Why prescribing is empirical and why that creates risk

When a clinician suspects a UTI, microbiological confirmation is typically unavailable at the point of prescribing. Urine culture and susceptibility results take hours to days to return; blood cultures are slower still (Werneburg & Rhoads 2022). Initial antibiotic selection is therefore empirical: the clinician chooses an agent based on local guidelines, the probable causative organism, and available clinical information. Guideline-based empirical therapy is designed to cover the most likely pathogens at a population level, but it cannot account for the individual patient's microbiological history.

A bug–drug mismatch occurs when the antibiotic selected is microbiologically inactive against the organism causing infection, as defined by in vitro susceptibility testing. Mismatch is a laboratory finding, not a direct measure of clinical outcome: some patients with a mismatch improve through host defences, while others deteriorate (Koh et al. 2023). Nonetheless, antibiotic mismatch carries real clinical cost. Among patients with uncomplicated UTI, treatment with antibiotics carrying higher local resistance rates was associated with a 50% greater risk of treatment failure and significantly shorter time to failure compared with lower-resistance options (Koh et al. 2023). In secondary care, inadequate empirical antibiotic therapy for bloodstream infection is consistently associated with higher 30-day mortality and prolonged hospital stay (Nguyen et al. 2021; Baltas et al. 2021).

## 2.4 Why going broad does not solve the problem

A prescriber aware of resistance uncertainty might choose a broader-spectrum antibiotic to reduce the risk of mismatch. This reduces mismatch risk at the individual encounter, but at a systemic cost. Antibiotic prescribing selects for resistance in individual patients, and the effect is greatest with broader-spectrum agents (Costelloe et al. 2010; Holmes et al. 2016; Rawson et al. 2021). Escalation to broader-spectrum agents when narrower alternatives would be adequate accelerates the accumulation of resistant organisms at the patient level and, through onward transmission, in the wider community and healthcare environment. It also exposes patients unnecessarily to the adverse effects of broader-spectrum therapy, including disruption of the gut microbiome and the risk of *Clostridioides difficile* infection (Rawson et al. 2021). The correct response to resistance uncertainty is not reflexive escalation but better-targeted empirical prescribing, informed by the best available patient-specific evidence.

## 2.5 Patient-specific resistance is predictable from prior testing

The key insight driving this trial is that individual patients carry substantial predictive information about their own future resistance. Prior antibiotic exposure and prior resistance in cultures are the strongest patient-level predictors of resistance at a subsequent episode. In five studies of urinary tract bacteria (n = 14,348 participants), the pooled odds of resistance were 2.5-fold higher within two months of antibiotic treatment and 1.33-fold higher within 12 months (Costelloe et al. 2010). A large cohort study linking over 700,000 community-acquired UTIs with individually resolved antibiotic purchase records demonstrated that prior urine culture resistance profiles and prior drug purchases together substantially reduce the risk of mismatch compared with standard population-based prescribing (Yelin et al. 2019). Further work confirms dose-response relationships: more antibiotic courses in the preceding 12 months are associated with progressively higher odds of resistance, and prior resistant isolates predict future resistance with odds ratios of 2.62 or higher (McCowan et al. 2022; Ciaccio et al. 2024; McDonnell et al. 2025). Recent exposure carries the greatest risk, but the signal remains detectable for up to 12 months and in some cases beyond (Costelloe et al. 2010; Yelin et al. 2019).

The clinical and policy implications follow directly. If a patient has previously grown an *Enterobacterales* isolate resistant to the antibiotic being prescribed, that historical result provides actionable information not captured by population-level guidelines. The 2025 IDSA guidelines for complicated UTI explicitly recommend avoiding antibiotics to which the patient has had a resistant pathogen isolated from the urine previously, and further recommend avoiding fluoroquinolones if they have been used in the preceding 12 months (Trautner et al. 2025). The evidence base for routinely acting on such information in clinical practice remains limited, and the practical barriers to doing so are substantial.

## 2.6 Patient-specific resistance is hidden by digital fragmentation

Patient-specific microbiological history exists, but it is distributed across laboratory information management systems and electronic health records scoped to individual provider organisations. A clinician at one NHS trust can generally access only results processed by that trust's laboratory. A patient who has previously attended a different hospital, or whose general practice uses a different laboratory, will appear to have no microbiological history — even when clinically relevant culture and susceptibility data exist elsewhere. Warren et al. demonstrated the scale of this problem using Hospital Episode Statistics for 2017–18: of 21.3 million patients with NHS hospital encounters, nearly four million attended two or more different trusts over a single year, and on 9.1% of all occasions patients presented to a hospital using a different EHR or paper record system to their previous attendance; only two of the twenty most common hospital pairs sharing patients used the same EHR system (Warren et al. 2020). A rapid evidence review of digital health fragmentation in the UK confirmed that this fragmentation adversely affects antimicrobial stewardship and prescribing, and that evidence specifically addressing the consequences for AMS remains limited (Schneider et al. 2026).

Pathology centralisation partly addresses this problem by creating a structural opportunity for cross-provider data aggregation. Lord Carter's 2016 review of operational productivity in English NHS acute hospitals recommended consolidation of pathology services into hub-and-spoke networks, with NHS Improvement subsequently planning up to 29 pathology networks across England (Lord Carter of Coles 2016). In North Central London, this consolidation is instantiated through Health Services Laboratories (HSL) — a partnership between The Doctors Laboratory, Royal Free London NHS Foundation Trust, and UCLH — which processes microbiology for all five NCL acute trusts (UCLH, Royal Free London, Barnet and Chase Farm, North Middlesex University Hospital, and Whittington Health) as well as community providers across the same footprint. Because HSL acts as the regional processing hub for primary and secondary care specimens alike, an interface built at the laboratory level provides a single point of access to microbiology results generated across the full care continuum for this population.

## 2.7 Patient-specific resistance is also a workflow problem

Centralised data access is necessary but not sufficient. Even where a laboratory portal or separate clinical system is technically accessible, information that sits outside the prescribing workflow imposes a retrieval cost that clinicians under time pressure cannot reliably absorb. NHS England's digital vision for antimicrobial stewardship explicitly recognises this: it calls for previous microbiology results to be available within a single, easily accessible interface at the point of care, and for interoperability between electronic prescribing systems and laboratory information management systems to be prioritised (NHS England, n.d.). A prescriber writing an antibiotic order in a busy assessment unit is unlikely to pause, open a separate system, navigate to the correct patient record, locate historical culture data, and mentally cross-reference resistance patterns against the drug under consideration — not through negligence, but because the prescribing context does not support it. The information must be presented at the point of decision, in a format that reduces cognitive load, and linked directly to the prescription being written.

## 2.8 The RADIX-BDM intervention

RADIX (Real Time Antimicrobial Digital Information eXchange) is a digital platform developed in partnership with Health Services Laboratories, the regional pathology hub for North Central London, serving a population of 1,749,195 across the London boroughs of Barnet, Camden, Enfield, Haringey, and Islington.^1^ RADIX aggregates historical microbiology data from across the NCL region using interoperable data standards including HL7 FHIR, and a minimum viable product has been integrated directly into the UCLH electronic prescribing environment.

RADIX-BDM is the specific alerting function under evaluation in this trial. It operates as follows. When a clinician initiates an antibiotic prescription for a urinary indication within Epic, this triggers a FHIR message sent to a central server at HSL. HSL searches its laboratory information system for AMR results for that patient, filters these to resistance relevant to the antibiotic being proposed, and returns the result to the prescribing clinician within milliseconds. Where a qualifying historical bug–drug mismatch is identified, the result is displayed as an interruptive alert within the Epic CPOE environment, showing the relevant organism, resistance pattern, sample date, and originating trust. The alert presents information only; it does not override the prescription or require any specific action. The clinician decides whether to proceed with the order, change it, or remove it.
